Move photos and files from Android to a Mac without a cable

Four ways that work, and when each one is worth it

Android and macOS were never designed to talk to each other. AirDrop only works between Apple devices, and Quick Share only works between Android, ChromeOS and Windows. So the most common pairing in a lot of households — an Android phone and a MacBook — is the one with no built-in way to pass a file across.

There are four realistic ways around that. None is best in every situation, so this guide goes through what each one costs you.

1. Over Wi-Fi, in the browser

This is what LocalShare was built for. Both devices need to be on the same Wi-Fi network.

  1. On the Mac, open www.local-share.com in Safari, Chrome or Firefox.
  2. On the phone, open the same address in Chrome. The counter at the top of the page should now say two devices are connected.
  3. On the phone, tap Share Files. Android's file picker opens; switch to the Photos or Gallery view if you want pictures, and select as many as you like.
  4. On the Mac, the files show up under "Shared by Others". Tick "Select All" and click Download. They land in your Downloads folder.

Keep the phone's screen on and the browser in the foreground until the progress bars finish. Android pauses pages in the background to save battery, and the phone is where the data is coming from.

Good for: anything from one photo to a few hundred, with original resolution and metadata untouched. Less good for: a whole 60 GB camera roll in one go — for a full backup, a cable is still quicker and does not depend on a browser tab staying open.

2. By USB cable

A cable is the fastest link you have, but macOS does not mount an Android phone as a drive on its own. Android exposes its storage over a protocol called MTP, which Finder does not speak. Google's own Android File Transfer app used to fill that gap and is no longer maintained; several third-party apps (OpenMTP is a free, open-source one) do the same job.

The steps are: plug in, pull down the notification shade on the phone, tap the "Charging this device via USB" notification and switch it to File transfer, then open the MTP app on the Mac. If nothing appears, the cable is the usual culprit — many cables bundled with chargers carry power only.

Good for: very large transfers and full backups. Less good for: a quick one-off, since it needs software installed on the Mac and the right cable to hand.

3. Through a cloud service

Google Photos, Google Drive, Dropbox and similar services all work: upload from the phone, download on the Mac. It is the option most people fall back on, and it is perfectly fine for a handful of documents.

The cost is time and, sometimes, quality. Every file is uploaded to a data centre and then downloaded again, and home upload speeds are usually a fraction of download speeds. A 2 GB video that crosses your Wi-Fi in a minute or two can take half an hour to upload. Google Photos also re-compresses photos and video if the account is set to "Storage saver" rather than "Original quality", which is easy to miss.

Good for: files you want to keep in the cloud anyway, or devices that are not in the same place. Less good for: large videos, or anything you would rather not put on someone else's server.

4. Bluetooth

macOS can receive files over Bluetooth if you turn on Bluetooth Sharing in System Settings under General → Sharing. It needs no network at all, which is its one real advantage. It is also slow — think a few seconds per photo and many minutes for a short video — and pairing can be temperamental.

Good for: a single small file when there is no Wi-Fi. Less good for: everything else.

Which one to pick

  • A few photos or a video, both devices at home: the browser route. Nothing to install, full quality.
  • The whole phone, before a reset or an upgrade: a cable and an MTP app.
  • The Mac is somewhere else: a cloud service, with the upload quality checked first.
  • No network at all: Bluetooth, and patience.

If the two devices do not see each other

The usual reasons are that the phone has quietly dropped to mobile data, that one device is on a guest network, or that a VPN is switched on. The guide on devices that cannot find each other walks through each of these.
