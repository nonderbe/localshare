Send a video without it being compressed

Why clips arrive blurry, and how to keep the original

You film something in 4K, send it to a group chat, and what arrives on the other side looks like it was recorded a decade ago. Nothing went wrong: the app did exactly what it is designed to do. This guide explains why, and how to get the original file across instead.

Why apps compress video

A minute of 4K video from a current phone is typically 300 to 400 MB; at 1080p it is still 100 MB or more. Messaging services deliver billions of clips a day, mostly to people on mobile data, so they re-encode video to a small fraction of its original size before it leaves your phone. The resolution drops, the bitrate drops much further, and fine detail — grass, water, hair, anything in motion — turns to smudge.

Email has a different problem: most providers reject attachments above about 25 MB, which is a few seconds of 4K.

How to tell whether a video was compressed

Compare the file size on the sending and receiving device. In the gallery or file manager, open the details or info panel for the clip. If the original was 350 MB and the received copy is 18 MB, it was re-encoded. The resolution shown in the same panel is the other giveaway.

Option 1: send it as a file, not as a video

Most messaging apps only compress things sent through the photo and video picker. If you attach the same clip as a document or file, it goes through untouched.

  • WhatsApp: tap the attachment icon, choose Document, then browse to the video. The "HD" toggle in the normal media picker is better than the default but still compresses.
  • Telegram: choose File in the attachment menu, or turn off compression when selecting media.
  • Signal: attach through the File option rather than the gallery.

Each service has its own size ceiling for documents, commonly around 2 GB, and the recipient needs enough mobile data or Wi-Fi to download the full file.

Option 2: a cloud link

Upload the clip to Google Drive, iCloud, OneDrive or Dropbox and share the link. The file is stored as-is. Two things to watch: photo-oriented services may have a "storage saver" mode that re-encodes uploads, and the upload itself can be slow — a 2 GB file on a typical 20 Mbit/s upload takes around fifteen minutes.

This is the right choice when the recipient is somewhere else.

Option 3: a direct transfer on the same network

If the device you are sending to is in the same building — your own laptop, a partner's phone, the family computer — there is no need for the file to leave the house at all. A local transfer copies the exact bytes, so there is nothing to compress, and it runs at Wi-Fi speed rather than upload speed.

With LocalShare: open the page on both devices, tap Share Files on the phone, pick the video, and download it on the other device. AirDrop (Apple to Apple) and Quick Share (Android to Android or Windows) do the same job within their own ecosystems.

Option 4: a cable or memory card

For a day's worth of footage, plugging the phone into a Windows computer is still the fastest route. Between an Android phone and a Mac a cable no longer works. Phones with USB-C can also write straight to a USB stick or SSD from the Files app, which works whatever computer the files end up on.

Things that look like compression but are not

  • HDR video looking washed out. Clips recorded in HDR can appear grey or over-bright on a screen or player that does not support it. The file is intact; the playback device is the limitation.
  • HEVC not playing. Newer phones record in HEVC (H.265). Older computers may show a black frame or refuse to open it without an extra codec. Again, the file is fine.
  • "Optimised" storage. If your phone offloads originals to the cloud to save space, the copy on the device may be a smaller preview. Download the original first, then send it.

Summary

If quality matters, never use the default media picker in a chat app. Send as a document, share a cloud link for people far away, or copy the file directly when the other device is on the same network.
